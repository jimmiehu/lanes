# River walks — notes for the essay

Working notes for an essay about walking along rivers in cities: what a river is for once nobody needs it for anything, and what a walk along one teaches that a map does not. Two cities, Vienna and Oslo, because those are the two I walked in this year.

This is the long note in the sample. The outline beside it lists its sections; a click on one goes there, and the mark in the margin follows as you read. ⌘F finds inside it, in READ and in WRITE.

## Rules for the walks

1. Walk with the current, never against it, unless the path forces it.
2. No headphones.
3. Write at the end of the walk, not during it.
4. Stop at every bridge and look both ways.
5. Note what the river is being used for, if anything.
6. Turn back when the path leaves the water, not before.

## The question

A river in a city used to be infrastructure: a road for goods, a drain, a source of power, a border. Most of those jobs have gone elsewhere. What is left is a line through the city that nobody planned as a whole, and a path beside it that somebody, at some point, decided people could walk on.

The essay asks what that path is for. Not what it was built for — most of it was not built for anything — but what it does now, to the people who walk it.

My first answer, before the walks: it is the only place in a city where you can walk for an hour without deciding where to go. The river decides. Everything else in a city is a choice of turnings.

Second answer, after the first three walks: it is where a city shows its back. The fronts of buildings face streets. Rivers get the yards, the loading bays, the pipes, the walls with no windows.

Third answer, still forming: it is where you can see the city's age in one look, because the river was there before everything else and everything else had to deal with it.

## Walk one — the canal, upstream

### Route

From the bridge by the old market, up the canal to where it leaves the main river. About six kilometres. Vienna, a grey Saturday in March.

### What I saw

- A path at water level, below the street, and a second path at street level above it. Two cities, one on top of the other.
- Graffiti on every wall at the lower level, layered so thickly the original paint is a rumour.
- A rowing club's boathouse, closed, with a list of members' names in the window going back decades.
- Herons, three of them, standing as if they had been put there.
- A man fishing who said he had never caught anything and did not expect to.
- Every bridge a different design, as if each had been commissioned without looking at the last.
- Bars on boats, shut for the winter, chained to the wall.
- At the top end, where the canal leaves the river, a lock gate and a keeper's hut with a light on.

### What it is for in the essay

The two levels. The street above is for going somewhere; the path below is for being somewhere. You can hear the traffic from the lower path and not see it, which makes the traffic sound like weather.

## Walk two — the small river, where it goes under

### Route

Along the small river that gives the city its name, from the park where it is still open to the point where it disappears under the road. Vienna, April.

### What I saw

- A river in a stone bed far too big for it. Most of the year it is a stream in a channel built for a flood.
- Railings with ornament on them, from a time when a drain was worth decorating.
- A railway beside it for long stretches, the two built as one project.
- The place where it goes under: a dark arch, a grille, and the sound of water going on without you.
- Steps down to the bed, locked.
- Children throwing sticks in at the park end and running to see them come out the other side of the footbridge.

### What it is for in the essay

The flood channel is the best image I have for the whole essay. Built for the worst day in a hundred years, used for the other thirty-six thousand as a place to look at a trickle. The city is sized for the river's tantrums, not its habits.

Draft sentence: *The bed is the river's memory of itself; the water is what it does now.*

## Walk three — from the lake down

### Route

The river in Oslo from the lake at the top of the city down to the fjord. About eight kilometres, all downhill, all with the current. May.

### What I saw

- A lake with a dam, and below the dam the river already in a hurry.
- Woods for the first stretch, so that the city arrives gradually: a fence, a path, a bench, a house, a street.
- Waterfalls, more of them than a city river should have.
- Brick mills, one after another, every one of them now something else: flats, a school, offices, a museum.
- A swimming spot with a ladder bolted to the rock, in use in May by people braver than me.
- Bridges low enough to touch.
- At the bottom, the river goes under a road and comes out at the fjord as if embarrassed by the whole thing.

### What it is for in the essay

The mills. The river was the engine of that half of the city, and now it powers nothing, and the path runs past the buildings it used to drive. It is the clearest case of a river that was infrastructure and is now scenery.

The swimmers matter too. A river nobody needs is a river people can get into.

## Walk four — the mills, slowly

### Route

The same river, the middle stretch only, walked again a week later and slowly, reading every sign. Oslo, May.

### What I saw

- A sign at nearly every mill, with a photograph of the workers in front of it, and a date.
- The workers in the photographs are mostly women and girls.
- The falls were the reason for the mills: the drop is the power. The signs say so, more or less, and the water says it louder.
- A footbridge with a plaque to a bridge that stood there before it.
- New flats with balconies over the water and nobody on the balconies.
- A café in a turbine hall, with the turbine left in as furniture.

### What it is for in the essay

The signs make the past legible, but they also make it finished. *This was a mill.* Past tense, framed. The river does not know it is in the past tense; it is doing exactly what it did.

Question for later: is a river path a museum? Something about walking past labels.

## Walk five — the long island

### Route

The new channel of the big river, and the long thin island between it and the old one. Vienna, June, a hot Sunday.

### What I saw

- An island that is entirely made: dug out of the flood plain and piled up, so that the river has somewhere to go when it rises.
- Half the city on it, apparently, on a Sunday in June: swimming, cycling, grilling, lying very still.
- Long straight banks, engineered, softened by thirty or forty years of willows.
- Signs about where swimming is allowed, ignored with confidence.
- A railway bridge overhead, and every few minutes the sound of a train arriving from somewhere else.

### What it is for in the essay

The flood defence that became the city's beach. Nobody voted for a beach; they voted, or somebody did, for not drowning. The beach is what grew on the defence.

This is the hopeful version of walk two. The flood channel in the small river is grim; the flood island in the big one is a holiday.

## Walk six — the fjord edge at dusk

### Route

Along the waterfront in Oslo from the old fortress to the new opera house and on east, past the end of the path. June, late, light until nearly eleven.

### What I saw

- No river at all, strictly: this is where the rivers end.
- A roof you can walk on, sloping into the water.
- Cranes, the working kind and the decorative kind, hard to tell apart.
- People sitting on the edge with their feet over the water, facing out, nobody facing the city.
- Further east, the path gives up and the port takes over: fences, containers, a sign saying private.

### What it is for in the essay

The end of every river walk is the moment the water stops being a line and becomes an edge. The walk has a direction and then it does not; you are left facing out.

The fences matter. The path is public until the water is still useful to somebody, and then it is not.

## Walk seven — the weir

### Route

Upstream from the allotments at home to the weir and back. An hour each way, in October, after a week of rain.

### What I saw

- The weir: a concrete step across the river, two metres of drop, water going over it in one sheet the colour of tea.
- The noise. You hear it a long way before you see it, and once you are there you cannot hear anything else.
- Foam in the pool below, turning slowly, collecting leaves.
- A fish ladder at the side, which I have never seen a fish use.
- A heron, of course.
- A sign about the weir's history, half covered in moss.
- On the way back, the allotments from the river side: the back of every plot, sheds and compost and water butts, the gardens showing their backs as the buildings do in the cities.

### What it is for in the essay

The weir is where a river is told what to do and does it, loudly. Everything before it is the river's; everything after it is ours.

And the allotments from behind are the same observation as walk one at a smaller scale: rivers get the backs of things.

## Walk eight — in the rain

### Route

The canal again, the same route as walk one, in heavy rain, to see what changes. Vienna, September.

### What I saw

- Nobody. Nobody at all at the lower level for the first two kilometres.
- The river higher and faster and browner, and the path in places only just above it.
- The graffiti darker, wet, easier to read.
- Every drain in the wall running, so the canal wall becomes a row of small waterfalls.
- The upper street busier than ever: people in cars, people under umbrellas, people going somewhere.

### What it is for in the essay

The path empties in the rain and the river fills. The two are not on the same side. The river is never more itself than when nobody is walking beside it.

## Walk nine — at night

### Route

The middle stretch of the Oslo river again, after dark, from the mills to the bottom. August.

### What I saw

- Lights on the falls, coloured, which I could have done without.
- More people than I expected, and all of them quieter than in the day.
- The water louder at night, or seeming to be.
- Bats over the pools.
- A bridge with a single lamp, and under it a man reading a newspaper.

### What it is for in the essay

At night the path stops being scenery and becomes a route again: people use it to get home. The river becomes infrastructure for an hour — not for goods, for people on foot who want the quiet way.

## Walk ten — the last one, with company

### Route

The small river in Vienna once more, with Ada, who knows it better than I do, from the park to where it goes under and then on above it, following where it must be.

### What I saw

- Things I had missed on walk two: a gauge on the wall for the water level, marked in metres, with the high-water marks of particular years painted beside it.
- A café on the bank that Ada says has been there longer than anyone can remember.
- Above the covered stretch, a market, a road, and nothing at all to say there is a river underneath.
- Ada stopping at a grating in the pavement and saying: listen. You can hear it.

### What it is for in the essay

The ending, probably. A river you cannot see but can hear, under a market. The city built over it and the river went on. You find it again by listening at a grate.

## Walk eleven — the canal, downstream

### Route

The canal from the old market downstream to where it rejoins the main river, past the end of anything a visitor would walk to. Vienna, October, a clear cold morning.

### What I saw

- The path keeps going long after the city stops trying to make it pleasant.
- A power station on the far bank, with a chimney, still working.
- Barges moored two deep, with washing on one of them.
- A sewage works, announced by its smell well before its sign.
- Sports grounds on the flood plain, empty at nine in the morning.
- Allotments, again, with their backs to the water.
- A ferry, a small one, on a cable, which I did not take.
- Where the canal meets the river, a bench, and on it two people watching the water go by as if it were a programme.

### What it is for in the essay

The working river. Upstream the canal is scenery; downstream it is still doing jobs — cooling, carrying, taking away. The path does not change, but what it walks past does. It is the counterweight to the mills: a stretch where the river has not been retired.

Question: does the essay need a walk where the river is still useful? I think it does, or the whole thing is an elegy, and rivers are not dead.

## Cut, but kept

Paragraphs that were in a draft and are not now. Kept here because a cut paragraph sometimes comes back.

### On maps

On a map the river is a blue line and the path is not there at all, or is a dotted line beside the blue one. The map is right about the river and wrong about the path: the path is the thing you can actually do. A map of a city's rivers is a map of the city's oldest decisions, and none of them were about walking.

### On bridges

Every bridge is a decision about where crossing is allowed, made by somebody who is not you, often a long time ago. A river walk is a walk from decision to decision. Between bridges the far bank is as far away as another city.

### On herons

There is always a heron. I have stopped writing them down, and then I write them down again, because each time it is standing as if somebody had placed it there for me, and each time nobody had.

## Draft paragraphs

### Opening

Every city I have lived in has a river I did not choose and a path beside it that I did. The river was there first; it is the oldest thing in the city that is still doing its job, if its job is to go downhill. The path is newer, and nobody can tell you quite when it became a path rather than the edge of something else.

### The middle

The walks taught me three things in order. That the river shows you the backs of things — yards, pipes, sheds, walls without windows — because a city faces its streets and turns its back on its water. That the river is sized for its worst day, and the city with it, so that most of the time you are walking beside a channel built for a flood and looking at a stream. And that a river nobody needs any more is a river people can get into: the mills are flats, the flood plain is a beach, and somebody has bolted a ladder to the rock.

### The ending

The last walk was with a friend who has lived beside the small river most of her life. Where it goes under the road she kept walking, along a street with a market in it and nothing to say a river was there. Halfway along she stopped at a grating in the pavement and said: listen. Under the stalls and the traffic you could hear it, going on, going downhill, doing the one thing it has always done.

## Words to avoid

- *Meander*, as a verb about people.
- *Artery*. A river is not blood.
- *Reclaim*, about paths. Nobody owned them first.
- *Liminal*. Just no.

## Notes on the notes

The walks are numbered in the order I wrote them up, which is neither the order they happened in nor the order they will appear in. The essay will probably open on walk one and close on walk ten and shuffle the rest.

Each walk got three headings: route, what I saw, what it is for. The third is the only one that will survive into the essay in any form. The first two are for me.

The list of what I saw is always longer than the essay can use. That is the point of writing it down: to have something to cut.

## Last walk planned

### Route

The canal at dawn, the whole length, before anyone else is there. November, once the clocks have changed and dawn is at a reasonable hour.

### Why

Every walk so far has been in the day or the evening, and in company with other walkers whether I liked it or not. I want one walk with nobody, and I want it at the time of day the river was busiest when it was still working.

### What to look for

- Who is there at dawn. Delivery drivers on the upper street, presumably. Anyone on the lower path?
- The light on the water before the sun reaches it.
- Whether the herons are already there.
- What the canal is being used for at six in the morning, if anything.

If this walk produces nothing, the essay ends at the grate.
